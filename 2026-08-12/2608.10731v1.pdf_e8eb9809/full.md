# When Is a General Factor Distinguishable? Non-Proportionality, Stable Structure, and the Bifactor Decision

Jinsong Chen Faculty of Education, The University of Hong Kong (jinsong.chen@live.com)

## Abstract

Whether an additional general dimension is necessary beyond correlated first-order factors is a property of the population covariance matrix, not of any estimator or design. This research establishes when that property can be decided. Where the general and group loadings are proportional within every cluster the bifactor structure is covariance-equivalent to correlated factors, so no sample size separates them (Proposition 1); where that proportionality fails in every cluster, three items per cluster and some mild regularities leave no �-factor model with diagonal uniquenesses able to reproduce the covariance matrix (Theorem 1); and between them lies a mixed boundary, located numerically here and turning on cluster resistance. Distinguishability is therefore graded, measured by the population distance to the �-factor class. Because that question is conditional on a first-order structure which is itself uncertain, a twostep procedure is developed within partially exploratory factor analysis, delivering a structure only when it reproduces across adjacent counts and treating non-delivery as legitimate. Simulation shows that a unanimous count can accompany a structure that fails to reproduce, and that absorbed local dependence can imitate a general factor,

the error growing with sample size while stability indicators stay clean. Four empirical datasets illustrate the possible outcomes.

Keywords: bifactor model; distinguishability; covariance equivalence; partially exploratory factor analysis; stable structure

## 1 Introduction

Few measurement models are as attractive and as distrusted as the bifactor model. Since its rediscovery (Reise, 2012), it has been fit to ability batteries, psychopathology inventories, and quality-of-life scales. It also wins the model comparisons it enters with remarkable regularity: more than 90% of the time against the higher-order model in mental-abilities batteries (Cucina & Byle, 2017). Yet the same literature documents chronic anomalies, with a reported prevalence above 60% in applications (Eid et al., 2017). These include collapsed or vanishing group factors, general factors that rotate into group factors, and loading patterns that defy interpretation. Methodologists have raised three further concerns. The bifactor’s fit advantage partly reflects its greater functional flexibility rather than structural truth (Bonifay & Cai, 2017; Murray & Johnson, 2013). The higher-order model is the bifactor plus a proportionality constraint (Gignac, 2016; Schmid & Leiman, 1957; Yung et al., 1999). Standard information criteria can prefer the wrong member of the pair at realistic sample sizes (Raykov et al., 2024).

This paper starts from one observation. These troubles are not independent pathologies. They are manifestations of one covariance-level fact: whether a general factor is empirically distinguishable from correlated factors is a property of the population loading pattern. Specifically, it depends on the proportionality between general and group loadings within clusters. When the pattern is proportional in every cluster, the bifactor covariance is exactly reproducible by a higher-order model, and the two classes are covariance-equivalent even when the parameters remain identified within a chosen class. When every cluster departs from proportionality, the classes are distinguishable at the covariance level (Theorem 1); a departure confined to part of the battery is not enough in general. Most real data sit somewhere between these poles, and where they sit determines how much an analyst can safely leave to the data.

Two literatures approach this problem from opposite identification philosophies, and both descend from Schmid and Leiman (1957). The exploratory lineage identifies an unrestricted loading matrix by rotation or constraint (Asparouhov & Muthén, 2026; Garcia-Garzon et al., 2019; Jennrich & Bentler, 2011; Jiménez et al., 2023; Qiao et al., 2025a): the analyst supplies only the factor count, anchor knowledge never enters estimation, and interpretation is post hoc. The partially exploratory lineage starts from the confirmatory pole: a few anchors are estimated structure rather than rotation targets, the remaining pattern is selected by regularization, and candidate structures grow from an anchored backbone through a factor-structure sweep (Chen & Jin, 2026a). The positioning diference is what each delivers. Exploratory hierarchical approaches condition on a supplied count and ask which representation is recoverable at that count. The present approach treats count uncertainty as part of the problem: its target is the stable structural core that persists across defensible adjacent counts, with no stable structure delivered as the explicit output when none exists, and the bifactor decision conditional on the delivered structure (Table 1). Relative to the framework’s own factor-selection machinery (Chen & Jin, 2026a), the increments are the distinguishability theory, the stable-structure estimand with its persistence indicators and explicit non-delivery output, the calibrated end-to-end evaluation, and the recast of the bifactor decision as a necessity question.

Separating the count from the structure matters in both directions. A unanimous count is not yet a deliverable structure: in Study 3’s cleanest population every replication agrees on the count, yet the selected structure still fails to reproduce across adjacent counts in 5.0% of them at � = 500, and the failure rate reaches 31.0% once interference is added; the bfi shows the same dissociation empirically. Conversely, count uncertainty need not mean structural uncertainty: adjacent counts can share a stable core, as the PID-5 data illustrate, and the comparison then proceeds at both counts.

That determination is the paper’s subject. In partially confirmatory factor analysis (PCFA; Chen et al., 2021; Chen, 2021; Jin & Chen, 2025a), and in its partially exploratory extension (PEFA; Chen, 2023; Chen & Jin, 2026a), the design matrix � carries what the analyst is willing to fix and leaves the rest to a spike-and-slab prior. PCFA supplies a full � over a stated count; PEFA launches from a backbone � and sweeps it over a window of candidate counts. Within either, specification depth is the uniform number of anchor items per factor the backbone carries; this paper works at depth 2 throughout, and deeper backbones are possible. Our central claim is that the cost of a free loading is contingent on reducibility: free loadings are essentially free when the population is irreducible with adequate loadings, and hazardous exactly where the population approaches reducibility, has silent clusters (clusters whose general and group loadings are exactly proportional), or is probed at the wrong first-order rank or with the wrong structural frame altogether. The reducible tier and its silent clusters fall outside the region where fully exploratory recovery is guaranteed (Qiao et al., 2025b, 2025a), and no existing study quantifies what partial specification buys in that excluded region; the Discussion returns to the neighboring exploratory and identifiability strands.

The paper makes three contributions. First, Section 2 gives the covariance-level core: Proposition 1 states that a reducible bifactor is covariance-equivalent to correlated factors, and Theorem 1 gives a suficient condition for the converse separation, with the mixed boundary and its consequences developed there; on this base the two-step process separates count screening from the operational bifactor comparison. Second, Section 3 reports three simulation studies keyed to the analyst’s three decisions, the anchoring design, sweep reliability, and the step-2 comparison, with the third study running the process end to end and calibrating its open specifics internally. Third, Section 4 applies the process to four empirical datasets spanning the evidence spectrum. Note that the simulation domain is deliberately bounded, with fixed five-cluster designs, correct anchors, and designed interference chosen to isolate the reducibility tiers; Section 5 states the corresponding scope limits.

Each name denotes a design, not a model: an anchor-zero design serves oblique and bifactor fits alike.

## 2.2 Distinguishability: two questions, one signal

The phrase “is the bifactor identified?” conflates two questions that the literature answers separately. Keeping them apart organizes everything that follows. We give them their proper names.

Identifiability is the estimation-side property: given that the orthogonal bifactor model is true, are its parameters uniquely determined by the covariance matrix, so that an estimator can recover them? This is a property of the model and its design: how many items per cluster, and which loadings are anchored. The question is settled essentially completely by Fang et al. (2021): for the standard (orthogonal) bifactor used here, identifiability holds if and only if every group factor has at least three items with nonzero group loadings and at least three clusters carry nonzero general loadings (or two, with a rank condition); for the extended model with correlated group factors, their suficient conditions count clusters whose general and group loadings are linearly independent within the cluster, exactly our within-cluster non-proportionality. When identifiability holds, anchored estimation is routine. The simulations below show it remains routine even in populations where the general factor is empirically redundant.

Distinguishability is the population-side property: is the general factor needed at all? That is, can the same covariance matrix be reproduced exactly by a �-factor model, so that no estimator, however good, could distinguish the two? This is a property of the population loading pattern, not of any design or algorithm. It is the Schmid–Leiman lineage’s question (Mansolf & Reise, 2017; Schmid & Leiman, 1957; Waller, 2018; Yung et al., 1999), and the one that matters for model choice. Identifiability asks whether the parameters can be estimated, given the structure. Distinguishability asks whether the structure can be chosen, given the population. No amount of data or estimation skill delivers distinguishability at the reducible boundary, because there is nothing in the covariance to deliver it with.

## 2.2.1 Covariance equivalence on the reducible set

Two covariance-level statements organize distinguishability, and one decomposition carries both. This subsection gives the first and Section 2.2.2 the second. Write $\mathbf { b } _ { g | k }$ for the restriction of the general column to cluster � (every cluster carries nonzero group loadings), and let Ψ denote the diagonal matrix of uniquenesses, so $\Sigma = C + \Psi$ . Within each cluster, decompose

$$

## 2.2.3 The mixed boundary

When some clusters are proportional, the theorem’s hypothesis fails and the rank budget acquires slack, and here the pattern of a non-proportional cluster decides its fate. Call cluster � resistant if it has at least four items and its within-cluster ratio vector $\rho _ { i } = b _ { s , i } / b _ { g , i }$ contains two disjoint unequal pairs; equivalently, if $\rho ^ { k }$ is not constant after deleting at most one entry. Numerical analysis (Table 11, reported with the proof in Appendix A) locates the boundary: a lone non-proportional cluster with only three items, or with a constantexcept-one ratio pattern, is traded away into the uniquenesses exactly when the remaining clusters are proportional, while a lone resistant cluster is not. A formal characterization of the mixed case remains open; the configurations relevant to this paper’s populations are verified numerically only.

## 2.2.5 Four questions, one bridge

Everything that follows rests on keeping four questions apart. Identifiability asks whether a specified model’s parameters can be recovered; count selection asks which candidate counts deserve examination; structural stability asks whether approximately the same configuration persists across adjacent counts on the observed data; and distinguishability asks whether $D _ { K } ( \Sigma ) > 0$ , so that an added dimension is required to reproduce the covariance. The middle two are logically independent, since a unanimously selected count can carry a structure that fails to persist and adjacent counts can share a persisting core. Step 2 poses the last question conditionally on the delivered structure, as evidence about the population property rather than an estimator of it. Nor do the four collapse: P5 is identified yet covariance-equivalent to a higher-order model, a stable structure can sit near the reducible boundary, and an undercounted or locally dependent structure can manufacture an apparently necessary general factor. Stable structure is the bridge between estimation and the bifactor comparison, neither a consequence of identifiability nor proof of distinguishability.

## 2.3 Estimation and operational criteria

All fits use the variational Bayes estimator of the PCFA framework, implemented in vbpm (Chen & Jin, 2026b; Jin & Chen, 2025a), which spans the confirmatory–exploratory continuum through regularization on the −1 entries (Chen, 2020, 2021, 2022, 2023; Chen et al., 2021; Jin & Chen, 2025b). Estimation runs on the warm-started path of the framework’s sparse-residual extension (Jin et al., 2026), and every unspecified loading receives a posterior inclusion probability, hard-selected at ≥ .5. Three criteria then serve three bounded roles.

The scale-free gain rule over the variational objective selects the first-order count (Chen & Jin, 2026a). Variational BIC, evaluated at the variational estimate under hard selection with the efective parameter count defined as the Jacobian rank of the implied covariance, a counting that remains meaningful at boundary configurations, is the operational criterion of step 2; $\Delta \mathrm { E L B O }$ compares each model’s own evidence bound and is an algorithmic sensitivity only, since its cross-design comparability is not established. Conventional fit statistics are descriptive and never decide the count or the added dimension.

## 2.4 The two-step rule and process

Given data with suspected clusters and unknown structure, the process separates two decisions that are confounded in common practice: how many first-order factors the data support, and whether a general factor sits above them. The deliverable is a stable structure when one exists, with the count as an attribute of the structure it travels with. Uncertainty can run upward (the selected structure may persist inside a larger one) or downward (a smaller core may be the stable object), so the process reports where the structure is stable rather than defending a single number, and reporting that no stable structure was delivered is a legitimate output when none is stable enough. Nothing in the process consults the true or theoretical factor count.

The two sweep modes, formally. A factor-structure sweep launches from a backbone $Q _ { 0 } \ ( J \times K _ { 0 }$ with $K _ { 0 } < K$ ; AZ-type anchor rows, two anchors per backbone factor) and pads it with fully exploratory (−1) columns to reach each candidate count in a window $[ K _ { \mathrm { m i n } } , K _ { \mathrm { m a x } } ] , K _ { \mathrm { m i n } } \geq K _ { 0 }$ , bracketing the counts suggested by classical extraction methods (Braeken & Assen, 2017; Horn, 1965; Kaiser, 1960). In oblique mode, candidate � fits the correlated-factors model with � columns and free Φ. In bifactor mode, the same $Q _ { 0 }$ is used, a fully specified general column is added internally, and all factors are orthogonal; � counts group factors, so candidate � carries $K ^ { * } = K + 1$ columns, and the modes align candidate-by-candidate. In both modes the primary selection uses the scale-free gain rule over the variational objective: the largest single-step gain is normalized to 100, and the sweep stops when gains stay below 10% or 20% for two consecutive steps, with raw-ELBO, raw-BIC, and BIC-gain selections and the absolute fit trajectory recorded alongside (Chen & Jin, 2026a).

Step 1: the PEFA sweep, with backbones and stability analysis. The sweep is a partially exploratory factor analysis (PEFA): a PCFA design whose entries are mostly unspecified, evaluated over candidate counts from an anchored backbone (Chen, 2023; Chen & Jin, 2026a). Its inputs are item-level data, a cluster hypothesis, and two anchor items per suspected cluster, taken in instrument order unless substantive markers are prespecified. The target is not the common-factor rank of Σ: in an irreducible bifactor population no exact �-factor representation exists, so a rank-consistent criterion may legitimately select � +1 at large �. What separates the steps is the magnitude ordering of Section 2.2.4, where an omitted group factor produces block misfit an order of magnitude larger than the general dimension’s residual gap. Step 1 therefore targets the stable group-structure core, the configuration of first-order clusters that persists across adjacent counts, with the count as its attribute; the gain rule screens for that structure rather than estimating rank, and its validity rests on operating characteristics (Studies 2 and 3). The sweep runs in oblique mode only, over a window extending the classical extraction results but never below $K _ { 0 } ,$ rerunning wider whenever an operative selection lands within one step of the upper bound; the preliminary study shows why bifactor-mode sweeps cannot count. Both AO- and AZ-type specifications run as a check, and the stability analysis around the selected count always runs.

The stability analysis and its decision rules. The stability analysis compares anchored solutions at adjacent counts, with the backbone columns held fixed by position and each remaining column matched to its nearest counterpart under a common orientation; the minimum Tucker congruence over matched columns is the binding index (i.e., the weakest column fails first), with the matched-loading RMSD available as a near-equivalent check (Section 3.2.2), and reproduction is checked upward (i.e., whether the selected structure remains present at $\hat { K } { + } 1 )$ or downward (i.e., whether adjacent selections retain a shared core). Backbone and cut agreement are flags; adjacent-count reproduction is the direct evidence and always runs, because the candidate loading and inclusion-probability matrices and their transition diagnostics are computed and retained during the sweep. Sections 3.2 and 4.2 show that full agreement can coexist with failed reproduction. ## 3 Simulation study

The simulation program has three studies over a common set of populations. Study 1 conditions on the correct structure. Fitting at the true five-cluster design, it compares the anchored designs on recovery, selection, and anomalous solutions. It also poses the step-2 comparison at the true count under both criteria and both specifications. Study 2 asks how the structure is found and what happens when it is found wrongly. It examines how the two sweep modes behave under the selection rules and cuts, and whether the backbone’s anchoring changes the count. It also examines what an over-count does to the recovered structure, and what an under-count does to the general-factor decision. Study 3 runs the two-step process end to end on populations carrying model uncertainty, evaluating step-2 results conditionally on step-1 results within the same replications; the specifics deferred in Section 2.4 are derived there.

## 3.1 The preliminary study and the design choices it fixes

Before the process can be evaluated end to end, four design choices have to be settled: how deeply to anchor, which sweep mode carries the count, whether the backbone’s own anchoring changes that count, and what an over- or under-count does to the recovered structure. A preliminary study over six fixed populations settles them, and Appendix B reports it in full. Six findings carry forward.

The anchoring design matters only where estimation is already hard: anchor-only (AO) designs trail anchor-zero (AZ) designs slightly and both trail full-primary specification, but the gaps appear only under weak loadings at small � and on the reducible population, and the errors are omissions rather than false discoveries (Finding 1). At the generating count the step-2 comparison has favorable operating characteristics in both directions, favoring the added dimension where it exists and the �-dimensional representation where it does not (Finding 2). The oblique sweep counts well and the bifactor sweep does not, under either specification, because surviving columns’ free entries can absorb an omitted group factor’s within-cluster block (Finding 3). The two specifications count almost identically, so neither is uniquely trustworthy, and an apparent advantage for one of them proved to be an artifact of an over-restricted backbone rather than a property of the anchoring (Finding 4). On the moderate and reducible populations, when the oblique sweep errs it errs upward, and a � +1 solution still contains the � structure beside a thin surplus column (Finding 5); on the weak populations its errors run downward instead, which a stricter cut cannot repair (Finding 3), and a bifactor-mode under-count takes the structure down with it (Finding 6).

Together these fix the workflow used from here on: an oblique sweep under both specifications, the ELBO gain at the 20% cut as the primary rule, and the stability analysis run on the anchor-zero solutions, the choice Finding 7 revises once the two are compared end to end. What they do not settle is whether the process delivers under model uncertainty, which is the subject of the study below.

## 3.2 Conditional evaluation of the whole process under model uncertainty

Studies 1 and 2 evaluated the components of the process separately, on populations that are exactly what the models assume. Study 3 runs the two-step process of Section 2.4 end to end on populations carrying model uncertainty, records the step-1 stability indicators of every replication, and evaluates the step-2 results conditionally on them. No threshold is fixed in advance: the delivery rule is calibrated on a prespecified half of the replications, checked once on the untouched half, and the specifics left open in Section 2.4 are decided here.

## 3.2.2 Results and findings

Finding 7: the specification the structural check runs on decides whether a correct structure is delivered, and the anchor-zero design rejects correct structures on bifactor populations. Table 4 crosses the two specifications on the same replications. Three readings separate them. Delivery is higher under the anchor-only (AO) design than under the anchor-zero (AZ) design in every population but the oblique control, and the gap widens with interference and with �, reaching 69.0% against 10.0% on the compounded cell at $N = 2 0 0 0$ . Mean matched congruence at the true step, from $K = 5$ to 6, sits at .96 to .98 under anchor-only but at .46 to .74 under anchor-zero on every bifactor population, that is, below the calibrated $\varphi ^ { * } = . 8 5$ where the structure is correct by construction. Conditional step-2 accuracy is equal in 13 of the 21 cells, better under anchor-only in seven, and worse in one, the borderline doublet at $N = 5 0 0$ where 94.4% trails 97.8%; the seven include the adversarial cell at $N = 2 0 0 0 , 6 0 . 5 \%$ against 14.2%. The evidence therefore favors the anchor-only specification at both steps, the first two columns for the step-1 structural check and the third for the step-2 comparison.

The middle column identifies the mechanism as a property of the design rather than of the data. Restricting attention to the replications whose primary selection hit the true count and whose anchor-zero solution provably recovers the generating structure (archived truth congruence $\ge . 9 0 ; n = 3 , 6 2 1 )$ , any rejection is a false rejection of the check. On the bifactor populations the anchor-zero check falsely rejects 42.5% of them against the anchor-only check’s 2.4%, rising to 68.4% and 70.2% on the two doublet populations at $N = 2 0 0 0 ;$ on the higher-order and oblique populations the two agree, 5.0% against 2.2%. Note that the conditioning set is defined by the anchor-zero fit being correct, so the comparison is if anything generous to it: its own stability check rejects between a quarter and seven-tenths of the structures it estimated correctly. The reason is the one the theory supplies. Anchored zeros close the outlet through which an irreducible general dimension would otherwise be absorbed at $\hat { K } { + } 1$ , so the larger solution reorganizes the retained columns instead; populations without such a dimension are unafected, which is exactly the pattern observed. The anchor-only specification is therefore adopted throughout, for the step-1 structural check and for the step-2 comparison alike. Note that $\varphi ^ { * }$ was calibrated on the anchor-zero record, before this comparison was run, and is transferred here unchanged; the transfer is conservative in the relevant direction, since the anchor-only congruences at the true step sit well above it.

One conservatism survives the change and is worth recording. Even under anchor-only, delivery falls on the compounded cell at the largest �, from 91.5% to 69.0%, and the cost is non-delivery rather than a misleading delivery. Note also what these runs cannot establish: the archived truth scores grade the anchor-zero candidates, so the anchor-only check’s low rejection rate cannot be decomposed into correct acceptances and false acceptances. The claim here is one-sided, that the anchor-zero check demonstrably rejects correct structures at a high rate on bifactor populations, and it is suficient to prefer anchor-only for the classification without quantifying what anchor-only delivers.

Either loading-based index can carry the threshold in place of the congruence. Calibrated the same way as $\varphi ^ { * }$ and transferred once to the untouched half, the worst-column RMSD at .20 agrees with the frozen rule on 98.1% of calibration replications, and the aggregate RMSD at .10 on 96.7%, both delivering within a point of it at equal or better conditional accuracy. The worst-column index is the closer analogue, as its correlation with $\varphi$ min suggests, because both are governed by the weakest column. The practical implication is that the three indices are near substitutes rather than independent criteria, so reporting them together is a consistency check and the rule stays single-index.

## 5 Discussion

Selection is not delivery: the gain rule identifies a neighborhood worth examining, not by itself a structure worth interpreting. Study 3 makes the inference quantitative: a unanimous count coexists with a structure that fails to reproduce, in up to 31.0% of replications once interference is added, and the empirical bfi behaves identically. Behind it sits the paper’s through-line about the bifactor’s practical troubles: fit advantages that do not replicate, anomalous solutions, and spurious general factors. These are not estimator pathologies to be tuned away. They are surface expressions of a population-level property with a sharp suficient condition and a partly characterized boundary. Distinguishability lives in the within-cluster loading-ratio patterns. It dies at the reducible boundary that the higher-order model occupies (Proposition 1), it survives whenever every cluster departs from proportionality (Theorem 1), and mixed configurations require the finer pattern analysis developed in the Appendix.

Study 3 adds a second hazard with the same signature as the first and a diferent cause. Study 2’s under-count result showed a missing first-order factor manufacturing a general factor; Finding 9 shows absorbed local dependence doing the same at the correct count, with the error growing in sample size while every stability indicator stays clean. Both hazards share the mechanism the theory names: covariance that the fitted first-order structure cannot carry can flow into the general column, whether its source is a merged cluster or a residual doublet. The second is especially dificult in practice because count-level stability indicators are blind to residual dependence by construction. That result demonstrates that the hazard exists, not that any particular battery carries it: the doublet is designed in advance here, whereas in application a residual pair identified from the same data would carry the further problem of post-selection. Theorem 1’s diagonal-uniqueness assumption marks the boundary: where residual dependence is substantively plausible, model comparisons should be accompanied by transparent residual diagnostics, while calibrated sparse-residual extensions (Jin et al.,

2026) remain a priority for future research.

The neighboring exploratory program spans analytic bi-factor rotation (Jennrich & Bentler, 2011, 2012), its empirical-target refinements (Garcia-Garzon et al., 2019; Jiménez et al., 2023), constraint-based structure learning with recovery guarantees (Qiao et al., 2025a, 2025b), and an identifiability strand that fixes the pattern completely (Fang et al., 2021; Peeters, 2012). None crosses the anchoring design with reducibility. The recovery guarantees require, for every parent–child pair, that any two rows of the sub-loading matrix be linearly independent, which is pairwise non-proportionality and so stronger than the clusterlevel criterion used here; the reducible tier, every silent cluster of the mixed tier, and even theorem-covered patterns containing one proportional item pair therefore sit outside them, which is where partial specification earns its keep. The unification of second-order and bi-factor EFA (Asparouhov & Muthén, 2026) reaches the neighboring conclusion from the opposite side, on the same Holzinger battery analyzed here. The programs agree that the structures are near twins and difer in the question asked: a rotation-based unification asks what the solution looks like at a supplied count, while the anchored workflow asks which structure the data support and whether an added dimension is necessary, identification entering through the likelihood rather than a rotation criterion, which is what allows the question to be posed as a model comparison at all.

The practical recommendations can be stated as six choices. First, sweep in oblique mode rather than bifactor mode: the bifactor sweep under-counts and takes the stable structure down with it (Finding 6), while the oblique sweep errs upward when it errs at all and leaves the core recoverable (Finding 5). Second, use the anchor-only (AO) specification at both steps: the anchor-zero (AZ) design rejects structures it has itself estimated correctly on bifactor populations, 42.5% of them against anchor-only’s 2.4%, and its conditional step-2 accuracy is never better except in one borderline cell (Finding 7). Third, deliver on the joint criterion, the first adjacent step whose operative gain has fallen below the 20% cut and whose stability index clears its threshold, with any one of the three indices carrying the threshold since they are near substitutes $\left( \varphi \geq . 8 5 \right.$ , aggregate $\mathrm { R M S D } \le . 1 0$ , or worst-column RMSD $\leq . 2 0 )$ ; a structure that meets both halves is an L1 delivery. Fourth, when the delivered count and the persisting count difer, report the pair rather than choosing between them: that is an L2 core with count uncertainty, and the comparison is posed at both counts, as the PID-5 illustrates. Fifth, given L1 or L2, the step-2 anchored comparison is the operative way to ask whether a general dimension is necessary; it is a conditional comparison at the delivered structure, not an estimate of $D _ { K }$ , and a reversal across specifications or across counts is a reason to withhold. Sixth, when nothing is delivered (L3), report the non-delivery and treat local dependence as the first thing to examine, since absorbed residual dependence imitates a general factor precisely where no stable structure exists (Finding 9); do not fall back on a bifactor comparison at a count the data do not support.

A frozen-structure maximum-likelihood refit is deliberately not reported, for a structural rather than an empirical reason. Such a refit runs downstream of the variational selection, taking the hard-selected pattern as given, so it cannot recover what selection discarded and therefore cannot check the selection that produced it; reporting it alongside would present one method plus a reweighting of its own output as two. Note that a simulation contest would settle little either, since conditional on delivery the operational comparison is already correct in every non-adversarial cell and the one population with room to difer is adversarial by construction. What a refit inherits is the selection’s error profile, and omissions dominate false discoveries by roughly two orders of magnitude here, so quantifying that inheritance requires knowing the generating loadings and belongs to a separate study.

Limitations bound the claims. The simulations judge recovery against generating truth, and their populations use fixed loading patterns with four-item clusters and correct anchors, cho sen to isolate the reducibility tiers at the cost of the heterogeneity real batteries show; a single within-cluster doublet likewise remains a stylized form of the dependence they carry. The persistence threshold is accordingly calibrated within the same scenario families and checked on an untouched half, not externally validated, and the surplus-column diagnostics are descriptive rather than a delivery branch. Step 2 is a conditional, anchored, variational-BIC comparison at a selected structure rather than an estimator or formal test of the unrestricted distance $D _ { K }$ , and its margins depend on the comparison design’s anchoring design. These bound interpretation rather than demand further simulation here. Future work can study weak, incorrect, or misspecified anchors and cluster hypotheses; varying cluster sizes; calibrated surplus and residual diagnostics; post-selection and boundary-aware inference; full categorical estimation, for which partially confirmatory machinery already exists on the itemresponse side (Chen, 2020); calibrated sparse-residual extensions (Jin et al., 2026); what hard selection costs a downstream fixed-pattern analysis, since a refit conditioned on the selected pattern inherits its false negatives and so cannot check the selection that produced it; and how deep structural persistence should be required to run.

At the 20% cut the picture simplifies: the two gain rules converge and improve, ELBO gain reaching 97–100% on the moderate and reducible populations under both backbones with the P5 decline gone (97–99.5 at every �), and BIC gain rising with it under AZ (68–100) but not uniformly under AO, where it stays low on P1 and P3 at $N = 1 0 0 0$ (18.5 and 43.0; Table S3); only the weak populations are unmoved, because their errors are under-counts that a stricter cut cannot repair. The practical reading is simple: use the gain rules, prefer the 20% cut, read both gains together, and treat raw-criterion disagreement as the signal that the count is uncertain.

Finding 4: anchor-only (AO) and anchor-zero (AZ) backbone sweeps are close. This finding has an unusual provenance that we report openly. The original sweeps were launched from a backbone sliced out of the full five-cluster design, which silently carried twelve extra fixed zeros: the anchor items of the two out-of-backbone clusters, pinned to zero on the backbone columns. All twelve are true zeros, nothing in any fit index flagged the diference (the deterministic estimator exposed it through a later seed-for-seed discrepancy), and yet the efect was enormous and two-faced: the over-restricted backbone drove the oblique gain rule to near-perfection on the moderate and reducible populations (100% throughout) while collapsing it on the weak ones (0–1.5% at every �). The planned comparison, the clean AZ-type against the AO-type backbone on identical data, turns out to be nearly null: the two intended specifications agree within a few points in almost every cell (e.g., oblique gain on P2 at $N = 1 0 0 0 \colon 9 1 . 0 \ \mathrm { v s } \ 8 3 . 5 ;$ on P6: 99.5–100 for both), with no systematic winner, so consistency with prior practice (Chen & Jin, 2026a) is a suficient basis for choosing. What matters is the accidental arm: adding correct zero restrictions to a backbone is not a free action. Twelve such restrictions turned a serviceable rule into a bimodal one with no visible symptom separating the regimes. Selection behavior is a joint property of the rule, the design, and the population, and restrictions should be defended, not merely permitted, before they are imposed.

Finding 6: the bifactor sweep can under-count, and the stable structure fails with it. In bifactor mode no rule reaches 70% correct in any cell, and the gain rule sits at 0–34%. The errors run downward. On P6 the absorbed � = 4 is selected essentially always, and the empirical sweeps of Section 4.2 show the same lower-boundary behavior on the Holzinger battery. Unlike the oblique over-count, the under-count destroys the structure it retains. Refitting the mode’s $K = 4$ candidate on P1 samples, the minimum group-column congruence to the truth averages .35, with one true factor simply gone. The complement of this finding is the cost of the opposite error: an under-counted firstorder structure manufactures a general factor.
